A ten-minute guide

How to read a prediction market

Every number on this site comes from people putting money behind what they think will happen. This page explains what those numbers mean, how we check whether they are any good, and where they tend to go wrong.

What this site is not

The Calibration describes what markets are pricing and scores a simple model against them in public. It does not recommend investments, bets or trades, and nothing here takes your situation into account. Prediction markets are restricted or illegal in some countries, and you can lose everything you put into them. If you are making a financial decision, talk to a licensed adviser.

A price is a probability

On Polymarket, a share pays $1 if something happens and $0 if it doesn't. If a share costs 23¢, the market as a whole is saying there is roughly a 23% chance it happens.

Happens23%
Doesn't77%

A 23% outcome is not "unlikely to happen" in any comforting sense: it should happen about one time in four. If it never happened, that would be the market being wrong.

In events with many outcomes (an election with 20 candidates), each candidate is its own yes/no market, so the percentages need not add up to exactly 100%.

Odds

Cards also show odds, such as 4.35×. That is simply 1 ÷ probability: a 23% outcome pays about 4.35 times the stake if it happens. Odds and probability are the same information in two shapes. Big odds mean a small probability, never a hidden bargain.

Calibration: are the percentages honest?

A forecaster is well calibrated when things they call 70% happen about 70% of the time, things they call 20% happen about 20% of the time, and so on. You can only check this over many forecasts, never from one.

The site is named after this idea. Our Scoreboard keeps every call so anyone can check whether our numbers, and the crowd's, are honest over time.

The Brier score

The Brier score turns "how good were these forecasts?" into one number. For each forecast, take the gap between the probability and what happened (1 if it happened, 0 if not), square it, and average.

Forecast 80%, it happened(0.80 − 1)² = 0.04
Forecast 80%, it didn't(0.80 − 0)² = 0.64
Always saying 50%0.25 every time

Lower is better. 0 is perfect, and 0.25 is what you get by shrugging and saying 50% on everything. On the Scoreboard, "skill vs crowd" is our model's Brier minus the crowd's on the same questions: below zero means we were closer to reality than the market.

When the crowd gets it wrong

Markets are good forecasters on average, but they have known weak spots. These are the situations where a price deserves a second look:

  • Thin markets. Little money traded (low liquidity) means one trader can move the price a lot. Check the liquidity figure on each card.
  • Long-shot bias. Very unlikely outcomes, the 1–3% ones, tend to be priced a little too high, because cheap shares with big payouts attract people.
  • Distant deadlines. Money locked up until 2028 earns nothing meanwhile, so long-dated prices can sit away from the true chance.
  • Fast news. Right after a big headline, prices can overshoot before settling. Watch the 1h and 24h moves.
  • Unclear rules. Sometimes the price reflects doubt about how the question will be resolved, not about the event itself.

Our model, QEST

QEST is a deliberately simple, documented baseline, not AI. It looks at how prices have behaved and produces its own probability. When it disagrees with the crowd by a clear margin, the call is written to a public, append-only ledger and scored when the market resolves. Losses stay on the record.

The point is not to beat the market on any one question. The point is a public, checkable record of whether a simple model adds anything, and the honest answer may well be "not yet".
